Abstract

This document describes a threat model for the Recognized Entities specification. It identifies and analyzes security and privacy threats specific to ecosystems that use RecognizedEntityCredential resources to express recognition of entities and the actions they are authorized to perform, such as issuing or verifying verifiable credentials.

1. Introduction

Threat modeling is a vital part of specification development. This document provides a threat model for the Recognized Entities specification, which defines a data model for expressing that one or more entities are recognized to perform specific actions — such as issuing or verifying verifiable credentials — within a particular ecosystem.

This threat model follows the methodology described in the W3C Threat Modeling Guide and classifies threats using the STRIDE taxonomy. Threats are grouped into four categories:

  • Target Threats — threats the specification was designed to address.
  • Implementation Threats — threats that implementers must handle; the specification leaves the choice of mitigation open.
  • External Threats — real-world risks acknowledged as out of scope for this version of the specification.
  • Dependency Threats — threats inherited from technologies the specification depends upon.

1.1 Description

The Recognized Entities ecosystem enables a list publisher — such as an individual, a club or organization, a national business register, an accreditation authority, or an industry consortium — to issue a RecognizedEntityCredential (O1) asserting that one or more issuers are recognized to perform specific actions. A holder can acquire a dependent verifiable credential from such an issuer and present it to a verifier alongside the recognized entity credential, allowing the verifier to confirm both the integrity of the presented credential and the recognized status of its issuer.

The core challenge is that a verifier receiving a dependent verifiable credential from an unknown issuer must be able to determine, in an automated and cryptographically-verifiable way, whether that issuer is recognized within the relevant ecosystem. The Recognized Entities specification addresses this by providing a data model for publishing recognition information as a recognized entity credential, enabling a verifier to accept dependent verifiable credentials from issuers described by recognized entity list publishers.

For this threat model, we consider a minimal instantiation of the Recognized Entities ecosystem consisting of four roles: the List Publisher (E1) who issues recognized entity credentials, the Issuer (E2) who receives recognition and issues dependent verifiable credentials, the Holder (E3) who receives and presents those credentials, and the Verifier (E4) who validates the credential chain against a configured set of trusted list publishers.

Note

Note: We do NOT model potential attackers as first-class elements, as over-characterizing attackers can lead to analysis bias that is better avoided.

1.2 Data Flow Diagram for the Recognized Entities Ecosystem

The data flow diagram below illustrates the key elements and data flows in a minimal Recognized Entities ecosystem. The diagram shows how a recognized entity credential (O1) is issued by a list publisher (E1) to an issuer (E2), how the holder (E3) presents a dependent verifiable credential (O2) alongside the recognized entity credential to a verifier (E4), and how the verifier retrieves the published list from publisher storage (S1) to confirm the issuer's recognized status.

The following notation is used in the diagram description and dictionary:

  • E — External Entity (human or legal person outside the system boundary)
  • P — Process (running algorithm that transforms data)
  • F — Data Flow (movement of data between elements)
  • S — Data Store (persistent or ephemeral storage)
  • O — Data Object (self-contained, transferable artifact)
  • C — Contained System Boundary (collection of elements under a single authority)

1.3 Dictionary

E1 List Publisher An entity — such as an individual, club, organization, accreditation authority, national business register, or industry consortium — that issues recognized entity credetnials that assert that one or more entities are recognized to perform specific actions. The list publisher is the authority for any recognized entity credentials it issues.
E2 Issuer An entity — such as an individual, club university, trade exporter, or a conformity assessment body — that has been granted a recognized entity credential by a list publisher and uses it to issue dependent verifiable credentials to holders. This entity controls an identifier document, such as a CID or a DID, whose identifier appears as the credentialSubject.id of the recognized entity credential and as the issuer of the dependent verifiable credentials it produces.
E3 Holder A person or organization that receives a dependent verifiable credential from an issuer and presents it — optionally, together with the recognized entity credential — to a verifier. The holder stores credentials in a software application, such as a digital wallet, and controls what is disclosed to verifiers.
E4 Verifier An entity — such as an individual, organization, customs authority, trade finance lender, or a market surveillance authority — that receives a credential presentation from a holder and must determine whether the issuer of that credential is recognized within the relevant ecosystem. The verifier is configured with one or more URLs that identify the list publishers, and lists, that it considers authoritative.
P1 Create List The process by which a list publisher vets an issuer to determine whether they qualify for inclusion in a list, and, if so, creates and cryptographically signs a new RecognizedEntityCredential, applying the publisher's private key material to produce a verifiable proof.
P2 Publish List The process by which a list publisher makes a signed RecognizedEntityCredential publicly available, typically via HTTPS, so that verifiers can retrieve it.
P3 Configure Verifier The process by which a verifier is configured with one or more recognized entity list publishers — the URLs or DIDs of list publishers whose recognized entity credentials the verifier will accept as authoritative, or recognized entity lists.
P4 Credential Issuance The process by which an issuer signs and delivers a dependent verifiable credential to a holder. This process uses the issuer's private key material to produce a cryptographic proof over the credential. The process can optionally also result in the delivery of a recognized entity credential to the holder.
P5 Credential Presentation The process by which a holder assembles and transmits a credential presentation — containing the dependent verifiable credential and optionally a recognized entity credential — to a verifier.
P6 Validate Credential(s) The process by which a verifier checks the cryptographic proof of a presented dependent verifiable credential, resolves the issuer's recognized entity credential, and confirms that the issuer is recognized within a configured list. This process may involve fetching recognized entity credentials from external URLs or from holder-provided presentations.
P7 Update List The process by which a list publisher modifies an existing RecognizedEntityCredential — for example to add, remove, or revoke recognized entities — and re-publishes the updated credential.
F1 Identify Entity The data flow by which an issuer provides identity and vetting information to the list publisher's Create List process, so that the publisher can determine whether to include the issuer in a recognized entity credential.
F2 Issue Recognized Entity Credential The data flow by which a list publisher delivers a signed RecognizedEntityCredential to an issuer. This credential asserts that the issuer's DID is recognized to perform one or more specific actions within the relevant ecosystem.
F3 Issue Credential(s) The data flow by which an issuer delivers a signed dependent verifiable credential to a holder. The credential contains the issuer's DID as its issuer value.
F4 Present Credential(s) The data flow by which a holder presents a dependent verifiable credential — and optionally a recognized entity credential — to a verifier. This flow crosses a security boundary between the holder's system and the verifier's system.
F5 Retrieve List The data flow by which a verifier retrieves a recognized entity credential directly from the list publisher's server, either to confirm an issuer's current status or to obtain a more recent version of the credential than the one provided by the holder.
S1 Publisher Storage The persistent storage within the publisher system where recognized entity credentials are kept and made available for retrieval. This store is managed by the list publisher and is typically accessible to the public via HTTPS.
S2 Holder Storage The persistent storage within the holder's system where dependent verifiable credentials and recognized entity credentials are kept. This storage is accessible only to the holder's software within the holder system boundary.
O1 Recognized Entity Credential A cryptographically-signed data object issued by a list publisher to an issuer. It asserts that the issuer's DID is recognized to perform one or more actions — such as issuing or verifying specific types of verifiable credentials — within the ecosystem governed by the list publisher.
O2 Dependent Verifiable Credential A cryptographically-signed data object issued by a recognized issuer to a holder. The credential asserts claims about the holder and is signed with the issuer's private key. Its trustworthiness depends on the issuer holding a valid recognized entity credential (O1), making it a "dependent" verifiable credential.
C1 Publisher System The system boundary — typically a web server or DID-linked resource host — managed by the list publisher. It runs the Create List, Publish List, and Update List processes and maintains Publisher Storage (S1) from which recognized entity credentials are served.
C2 Issuer System The system boundary — typically a server or service operated by the issuer — that manages the issuer's credentials, keys, and DID. It runs the Credential Issuance process and holds the recognized entity credential issued to the issuer.
C3 Holder System The system boundary — typically a mobile or desktop application — that manages the holder's credentials, keys, and DID. All credential storage (S2) and Credential Presentation logic runs within this system boundary, which is under the holder's control.
C4 Verifier System The system boundary — typically an automated verification service or human-operated review system — that runs the Configure Verifier and Validate Credential(s) processes. It is configured with trust anchors identifying authoritative list publishers.

2. Stakeholders

2.1 E1 List Publisher

A list publisher is an entity that issues RecognizedEntityCredential resources. List publishers are the authority for any recognized entity credentials they issue. They range from national government bodies (such as business registers and accreditation authorities) to industry consortia and standards organizations.

List publishers have a strong interest in ensuring that only properly vetted entities appear in their lists, and that their list credentials are not forged or misused. They also bear operational responsibility for the availability and integrity of the credentials they publish.

2.2 E2 Issuer

An issuer is any person or organization that has been granted a RecognizedEntityCredential by a list publisher and uses that recognition to issue dependent verifiable credentials to holders. Examples include universities issuing academic credentials, national business registers issuing trade entity credentials, and conformity assessment bodies issuing product certificates.

The issuer's primary goal is to issue dependent verifiable credentials that will be accepted by verifiers within the relevant ecosystem. They want confidence that their recognized status is accurately represented, that their recognition credentials are not misappropriated or tampered with, and that their DID remains under their control.

2.3 E3 Holder

A holder is a person or organization that receives dependent verifiable credentials from issuers and presents them to verifiers. In the cross-border trade use case, the holder is an importer presenting an exporter's invoice credential to customs. In the education use case, the holder is a student presenting a university-issued diploma.

The holder's primary goal is to present credentials that will be accepted by verifiers, without disclosing more information than necessary. Holders have a strong interest in privacy: they do not want their credential presentations to leak information about their activities to list publishers or other third parties.

2.4 E4 Verifier

A verifier is an entity that receives a credential presentation from a holder and must determine whether to accept it. Verifiers range from automated customs clearance systems processing thousands of trade documents per hour to human-operated market surveillance authorities checking product conformity certificates.

The verifier's primary goal is to confirm that the credential it has received was issued by an entity that is genuinely recognized within the relevant ecosystem, and that the credential itself has not been tampered with. Verifiers also have a strong interest in availability: a denial of service against the lists they depend on directly impairs their ability to function.

3.2 Threat Details

Target Threats

3.2.1 T1. Unauthorized Issuer Spoofing

T1. Unauthorized Issuer Spoofing security

[Threat List]
An entity that has not been granted a RecognizedEntityCredential by a legitimate list publisher attempts to present itself as a recognized issuer. The attacker fabricates, replays, or reuses a stolen recognized entity credential to make their verifiable credentials appear to originate from a legitimately recognized entity. Without strict cryptographic verification of the full recognized entity credential chain — from the issuer’s credential back to a trusted list publisher — a verifier may accept credentials from actors who were never actually recognized. This is particularly dangerous in high-stakes scenarios such as cross-border trade finance or product conformity assessment, where a fraudulent issuer could cause significant economic harm.
R1. Cryptographic Verification of the Recognized Entity Credential Chain (Reduce)
Verifiers cryptographically verify that every recognized entity credential in a presented chain carries a valid proof, that the credentialSubject.id of the recognized entity credential matches the issuer.id of the credential under evaluation, and that the outermost recognized entity credential was issued by an entity within the verifier’s configured set of trust anchors. The specification requires conforming processors to produce errors when these checks fail, preventing unverified entities from appearing to be recognized.
Affected Components: F4: Present Credential(s), F5: Retrieve List, O2: Dependent Verifiable Credential, O1: Recognized Entity Credential
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Spoofing)

3.2.2 T2. Recognized Entity Credential Tampering

T2. Recognized Entity Credential Tampering security

[Threat List]
When a holder presents a bundle containing both their own verifiable credential and a recognized entity credential, a malicious intermediary could swap or modify the recognized entity credential, substituting one that makes an unrecognized issuer appear legitimate. For example, a transport layer intermediary could replace a valid recognized entity credential bound to Issuer A with one bound to Issuer B, effectively laundering an unrecognized issuer’s credentials through a legitimate recognition chain. Without integrity protection on the binding between the verifiable credential and its associated recognized entity credential, such substitution is undetectable.
R2. Cryptographic Binding Between Credential and Recognition (Reduce)
Recognized entity credentials are cryptographically signed by the list publisher using a verifiable proof. Verifiers confirm that the credentialSubject.id of the recognized entity credential exactly matches the issuer.id (or issuer value) in the presented verifiable credential. Any mismatch causes the verification to fail. Because each recognized entity credential is independently signed, tampering with the binding is detectable through standard proof verification.
Affected Components: F4: Present Credential(s), O2: Dependent Verifiable Credential, O1: Recognized Entity Credential
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Tampering)

Implementation Threats

3.2.3 T3. List Retrieval Denial of Service

T3. List Retrieval Denial of Service security

[Threat List]
When verifiers fetch recognized entity credential resources directly from external URLs, two classes of denial-of-service condition can arise. At the network layer, a surge of simultaneous verification requests can overwhelm the publisher system list host, turning the recognized entity credential list into a single point of failure; alternatively, a slow-drip server can return a response byte-by-byte, causing verifier processes to hang indefinitely and exhaust connection pools. At the data layer, a recognized entity credential whose list of recognized entities grows to an impractical size — either through legitimate growth or adversarial inflation — can exhaust memory or CPU when the verifier naively loads, parses, or searches the entire list during Validate Credential(s). High-throughput verification systems (such as customs authorities processing thousands of trade documents per hour) are especially vulnerable to both classes of attack.
R3. Credential Stapling, Caching, Timeouts, and Size Limits (Reduce)
Holders present recognized entity credentials directly to verifiers (“credential stapling”) rather than requiring verifiers to fetch them, distributing load away from the list host. When direct fetching is necessary, verifier implementations enforce both connection and read timeouts and enforce maximum payload size limits on all list fetches. Verifiers impose maximum list size thresholds and reject or skip lists that exceed configurable limits. Implementations use streaming or paginated access patterns rather than loading entire lists into memory. Verifiers cache resolved recognized entity credentials with appropriate TTLs aligned to the credential’s validUntil period. List publishers deploy content delivery network infrastructure, rate limiting, and paginated or indexed lookup mechanisms to allow verifiers to check membership without retrieving full lists.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E3: Holder, E4: Verifier, F5: Retrieve List, P6: Validate Credential(s), C1: Publisher System, C4: Verifier System, S1: Publisher Storage
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Denial of Service)

3.2.4 T4. Privacy Leakage via List Fetch

T4. Privacy Leakage via List Fetch privacy

[Threat List]
When a verifier fetches a recognized entity credential over the network to confirm an issuer’s status, the party serving that fetch learns that a verification event involving that specific issuer is occurring. The fetch is correlated with the verifier’s IP address and timestamp. In high-volume ecosystems with many issuers each referenced by a unique URL, the serving party can build detailed surveillance logs mapping which verifiers are checking which issuers at what times — revealing commercial relationships, transaction volumes, and business activity patterns that neither the issuer nor the verifier intended to disclose. Who learns this depends on where the recognized entity credential is retrieved from. Fetching from a list publisher’s server exposes the verification event to the list publisher. Fetching from a whois service endpoint declared in an issuer’s controlled identifier document exposes it to the issuer itself, which learns of each verification of its own credentials and cannot be mitigated by retrieving a larger list, because such an endpoint serves a single issuer by construction.
R4. Credential Stapling and Bulk List Retrieval (Reduce)
Deployments choose a delivery mechanism according to how much it discloses. Delivery by the holder discloses the least. Holders present the recognized entity credential directly alongside their verifiable credential (“credential stapling”), so no third party is contacted at verification time and no party other than the verifier learns that the verification occurred. Retrieval from a list publisher discloses more, and verifiers that retrieve the full recognized entity credential rather than contacting a per-issuer endpoint ensure that any single fetch reveals only that the verifier is interested in some entity in that list, not which specific issuer is being verified. Retrieval from a whois service endpoint discloses the most, because the endpoint serves a single issuer and that issuer therefore learns of every verification of its own credentials. When a verifier cannot avoid fetching, it reduces the exposure by caching retrieved recognized entity credentials for their validity period rather than fetching them per transaction, and by issuing the request through Oblivious HTTP [RFC9458], which separates the request from the verifier’s IP address so that the serving party learns which recognized entity credential was requested but not who requested it. Implementers are advised to use privacy-preserving techniques such as anonymous credentials or blind fetch proxies for particularly sensitive ecosystems.
Affected Components: F5: Retrieve List, C1: Publisher System
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Information Disclosure)

3.2.5 T5. Unvetted Recognized Entity Credential Propagation

T5. Unvetted Recognized Entity Credential Propagation security

[Threat List]
A holder who receives a recognized entity credential may share it with others without independently verifying its integrity or provenance. A malicious or negligent actor could craft a recognized entity credential that lists fraudulent or compromised entities as recognized issuers, then propagate it through peer-to-peer channels outside the Retrieve List flow. Verifiers who accept the credential without running Validate Credential(s) against an independently-established trust anchor may unknowingly accept dependent verifiable credentials from untrustworthy sources. The decentralized nature of the specification amplifies this risk: a single unvetted credential shared widely can rapidly corrupt the trust assumptions of many parties across an ecosystem.
R8. Independent Cryptographic Verification Before Acting on Any Recognized Entity Credential (Reduce)
Verifiers independently verify the cryptographic integrity and provenance of any recognized entity credential before acting on it, regardless of the channel through which it was received. The list publisher identified as the issuer of the recognized entity credential is validated against a trust anchor established through independent, out-of-band means — not through the credential itself. Verifiers do not treat peer acceptance of a credential as evidence of its validity. Verifiers also confirm that the credential has not expired and has not been revoked before relying upon it.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E3: Holder, E4: Verifier, F4: Present Credential(s), P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Spoofing)

3.2.6 T6. List Publisher Impersonation

T6. List Publisher Impersonation security

[Threat List]
A malicious actor could create a recognized entity credential that mimics a well-known list publisher by using a similar or confusable issuer identifier or display name. A verifier that does not independently validate the issuer identifier against a known root of trust during Configure Verifier could be deceived into treating an attacker-controlled list as authoritative. This is distinct from unauthorized issuer spoofing (T1): rather than an unrecognized issuer presenting fraudulent credentials, the attacker here fabricates the list publisher identity itself — causing the verifier to accept an entirely attacker-controlled recognition registry as a legitimate trust anchor.
R9. Out-of-Band Validation of List Publisher Identifiers (Reduce)
Verifiers validate the issuer identifier of any recognized entity credential against a pre-established set of trusted identifiers obtained through independent, out-of-band means before adding it to their configured trust anchors during Configure Verifier. Display names, logos, and human-readable labels are not sufficient — the cryptographic identifier (e.g., DID or HTTPS URL) is always verified. Governance frameworks publish canonical, authoritative identifiers for list publishers through an independently-secured channel so that verifiers have a reliable reference to check against.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E4: Verifier, P3: Configure Verifier, P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Spoofing)

3.2.7 T7. Stale Recognition Acceptance

T7. Stale Recognition Acceptance security

[Threat List]
A revoked or compromised issuer may continue to pass Validate Credential(s) through two related failure modes. First, a recognized entity credential with an excessively long validUntil period remains valid long after the issuer’s recognition has been withdrawn; a holder who cached the credential before revocation may continue to present it successfully. Second, even when list publishers set appropriate validity periods and publish timely updates via Update List, a verifier that caches aggressively or operates offline may continue using stale list data well beyond an acceptable freshness window, accepting credentials from issuers whose recognition has since changed. Both failure modes share the same impact — a credential that should fail validation passes — but require complementary mitigations at the publisher and verifier levels respectively.
R10. Risk-Proportionate Validity Periods, Revocation, and Cache Freshness Controls (Reduce)
List publishers select validity periods appropriate to the risk profile and operational capacity of the ecosystem. High-risk or large ecosystems prefer short validity periods (24–48 hours); smaller, lower-risk ecosystems might use longer periods provided robust revocation mechanisms are also deployed. List publishers provide revocation status endpoints that allow point-in-time queries rather than requiring full list re-fetches. Verifier implementations check the validUntil field during Validate Credential(s) and do not accept expired credentials. Verifiers respect cache-control directives from list publishers and enforce maximum cache TTLs appropriate to the ecosystem risk profile, performing fresh retrieval or live revocation checks when cached data exceeds the configured staleness threshold. Governance frameworks specify acceptable cache freshness windows for compliant verifier implementations.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E3: Holder, E4: Verifier, P1: Create List, P6: Validate Credential(s), P7: Update List, F5: Retrieve List
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Tampering)

3.2.8 T8. External Resource Substitution

T8. External Resource Substitution security

[Threat List]
A recognized entity credential may reference external resources — such as outputValidation schema files or governance documents — via URL. If those URLs are not integrity-protected, an attacker can substitute malicious content through any of three mechanisms: (1) compromising the hosting server directly and altering the files in publisher storage; (2) acquiring or hijacking the domain the URL resolves to (e.g., via domain expiry or sale), allowing the new controller to replace previously trusted content; or (3) poisoning the DNS cache used by the verifier during Retrieve List, redirecting resolution to an attacker-controlled server without the domain changing hands. In all three cases, verifiers running Validate Credential(s) who fetch and apply the substituted resource may accept dependent verifiable credentials that fall outside the intended schema, or reject valid ones, without any indication that the external resource has been tampered with.
R11. Content Integrity Protection, DNSSEC, and TLS Verification (Reduce)
List publishers use content integrity protection (such as digestMultibase) for all externally-referenced resources in recognized entity credentials. Because content integrity verification detects substitution regardless of whether the attack is a server compromise, domain takeover, or DNS hijack, it is the primary defense against all three mechanisms. Verifiers verify the integrity of any fetched resource against the declared digest before applying it during Validate Credential(s), and treat a digest mismatch as a hard failure. Verifiers use DNSSEC-validating resolvers and verify TLS certificates against expected values when fetching external resources. Where possible, schema files are embedded directly in the credential or hosted on infrastructure under the list publisher’s direct control within the publisher system. Any recognized entity credential that references external resources without integrity protection is rejected by conforming verifiers.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E4: Verifier, F5: Retrieve List, P6: Validate Credential(s), C1: Publisher System, S1: Publisher Storage
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Tampering)

3.2.9 T9. Unbounded Recognition Chain Trust

T9. Unbounded Recognition Chain Trust security

[Threat List]
The recognizedIn property allows recognized entity credentials to chain to other registries — such as ETSI Trust Service Lists, X.509 certificate authority lists, or other recognized entity credentials. A compromised or malicious entry in a lower-level registry could be transitively trusted if verifiers do not enforce depth limits or independently validate each link during Validate Credential(s). Unbounded transitive trust chains amplify the blast radius of any single compromised registry: an attacker who compromises a registry at any depth gains the ability to assert recognition claims that propagate upward through the entire chain to verifiers that have configured only a top-level trust anchor.
R12. Chain Depth Limits and Per-Link Validation (Reduce)
Verifiers enforce a maximum depth limit when traversing recognition chains via recognizedIn. Each link in the chain is independently validated for cryptographic integrity and issuer trust during Validate Credential(s) — transitive trust from a higher-level registry does not substitute for independent validation of each link. Ecosystem governance frameworks specify maximum permitted chain depths and enumerate the specific registry types that might appear at each level. Verifiers reject chains that exceed the configured depth or that include registry types not explicitly permitted by their governance configuration.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E4: Verifier, F5: Retrieve List, P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Elevation of Privilege)

3.2.10 T10. Abuse of Recognized Actions

T10. Abuse of Recognized Actions security

[Threat List]
The action property in a recognized entity credential is a free-form string (e.g., issue, verify). Without strict ecosystem-level governance of allowable action values, a issuer may present credentials claiming broad or undefined actions that verifiers interpret more permissively than the list publisher intended during Validate Credential(s). This can grant recognized entities capabilities beyond what was authorized — for example, a credential authorizing issue for one credential type being interpreted as authorizing issuance of any credential type. Overly permissive interpretation could enable credential forgery, regulatory non-compliance, or fraud.
R13. Controlled Action Vocabulary and Strict Interpretation (Reduce)
Ecosystem governance frameworks define and publish a controlled vocabulary of permitted action values and their precise semantics. List publishers use only values from the governed vocabulary when issuing recognized entity credentials. Verifiers accept only action values they explicitly recognize and have been configured to trust during Configure Verifier. Verifiers treat unknown, overly broad, or undefined action values as grounds for rejection during Validate Credential(s) rather than falling back to permissive defaults.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E4: Verifier, P3: Configure Verifier, P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Elevation of Privilege)

3.2.11 T11. Overly Permissive Schema Constraints

T11. Overly Permissive Schema Constraints security

[Threat List]
A list publisher who creates a recognized entity credential may specify outputValidation schema rules that are too broad, incomplete, or lax, inadvertently permitting recognized issuers to issue a wider class of dependent verifiable credentials than was intended. An issuer — acting legitimately within the stated schema constraints — could issue credential types that the list publisher did not authorize, or the missing constraints could be exploited by a malicious issuer to produce credentials that technically pass Validate Credential(s) despite falling outside the intended recognition scope.
R14. Precise and Complete Schema Validation Rules (Reduce)
List publishers define outputValidation schema rules that are as precise and restrictive as the intended recognition scope requires. Schemas enumerate permitted credential types explicitly rather than relying on broad or permissive patterns. Governance frameworks review and audit schema constraints as part of the Create List and Update List processes. Verifiers treat credentials that fail schema validation as invalid and do not relax validation rules to accept credentials that would otherwise be rejected.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E4: Verifier, P1: Create List, P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Elevation of Privilege)

3.2.12 T12. JSON Schema Attack

T12. JSON Schema Attack security

[Threat List]
The outputValidation property of a recognized entity credential references JSON schema files used during Validate Credential(s). A maliciously crafted schema — introduced by a compromised list publisher, an attacker who has tampered with a schema hosted at an unprotected URL (see T11), or via a crafted recognized entity credential — may contain pathological regular expressions or deeply nested structures that cause catastrophic backtracking or excessive memory consumption in the verifier’s JSON schema processor. This constitutes a denial-of-service attack against the verifier system itself. Even without malicious intent, poorly-written schemas can cause the same effect.
R15. Schema Complexity Limits and Safe Regex Validation (Reduce)
Verifier implementations enforce time and memory limits on all JSON schema validation operations performed during Validate Credential(s). Implementations use schema validation libraries that protect against regex denial-of-service (ReDoS) by using linear-time regex engines or by bounding pattern complexity. List publishers avoid complex or unbounded regular expressions in outputValidation schemas. Governance frameworks require schema review as part of the Create List process to catch pathological patterns before publication.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E4: Verifier, P1: Create List, P6: Validate Credential(s), C4: Verifier System
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Denial of Service)

External Threats

3.2.13 T13. Surveillance of Recognized Entity Identifiers

T13. Surveillance of Recognized Entity Identifiers privacy

[Threat List]
A recognized entity credential publicly names issuers with their identifiers, legal names, and other attributes. Aggregating these credentials across multiple lists enables systematic profiling and surveillance of recognized entities, including natural persons acting as issuers or verifiers. Because the credential is designed to be publicly retrievable via Retrieve List, any party that fetches it from publisher storage gains access to the full membership of the list. This risk is amplified when multiple lists are aggregated — cross-referencing identifiers across lists can reveal entity relationships, activity patterns, and ecosystem memberships that no single list discloses on its own.
R6. Minimize Personal Information in Recognized Entity Credentials (Reduce)
List publishers minimize the personal information included in recognized entity credentials for natural persons. Where feasible, pseudonymous or role-based identifiers are preferred over personally identifying information such as legal names and images. Governance frameworks establish policies on what attributes are permitted in a recognized entity credential and require a documented justification for any personally identifying fields. List publishers advise recognized entities that their inclusion in a publicly-accessible list is effectively a public disclosure of their participation in the ecosystem.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, S1: Publisher Storage, F5: Retrieve List
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Information Disclosure)

3.2.14 T14. Harms by Association

T14. Harms by Association privacy

[Threat List]
A recognized entity credential reveals the membership structure of the ecosystem it governs. Even in ecosystems intended to remain private, the credential may escape controlled distribution — for example, when a member of a private group publishes it externally, or when it is cached by intermediaries after being retrieved via Retrieve List. This exposes which entities participate in the ecosystem, which may be commercially sensitive, create competitive risks, or expose individuals and organizations to targeting, discrimination, or reputational harm. Unlike selective disclosure mechanisms, containment relies entirely on the honesty of all recipients — a single disclosure breaks the boundary for the entire list.
R7. Scoping and Distribution Controls for Sensitive Ecosystems (Accept)
The specification cannot prevent a holder or verifier from disclosing a received recognized entity credential to third parties. List publishers treat the act of publishing a recognized entity credential as equivalent to a public disclosure of the membership list. For highly sensitive ecosystems, list publishers consider issuing individual recognized entity credentials to each entity rather than a single list-style credential, limiting the blast radius of any single disclosure. Governance frameworks explicitly advise ecosystem participants that membership in a recognized entity list might become publicly known and that sensitive ecosystems might be better served by alternative trust mechanisms.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E3: Holder, S1: Publisher Storage, F5: Retrieve List
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Information Disclosure)

3.2.15 T15. Compromise of Recognized Entity Identifier

T15. Compromise of Recognized Entity Identifier security

[Threat List]
A issuer recognized in a recognized entity credential is identified by a cryptographic identifier (such as a DID or a public key). If an attacker compromises the private key material associated with that identifier — through phishing, insider threat, infrastructure breach, or cryptographic weakness — the attacker can impersonate the legitimate issuer during Credential Issuance. Verifiers performing Validate Credential(s) will see a valid cryptographic signature from a recognized identifier, and will have no basis to distinguish legitimate from fraudulent issuance. This is distinct from T1 (spoofing a non-recognized issuer) because the compromised identifier is genuinely enrolled in the registry.
R16. Key Rotation, Revocation Notification, and Hardware Security (Reduce)
Issuers protect signing keys with hardware security modules or equivalent tamper-resistant key storage. List publishers support rapid key rotation and revocation notification workflows so that a compromised identifier can be removed from recognition without waiting for scheduled Update List cycles. Governance frameworks require issuers to report suspected key compromise promptly and define incident response procedures. Verifiers subscribe to revocation feeds or check revocation status in near-real-time for high-value validation scenarios.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E4: Verifier, P4: Credential Issuance, P6: Validate Credential(s), P7: Update List
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Spoofing)

3.2.16 T16. Verifier Trust Anchor Misconfiguration

T16. Verifier Trust Anchor Misconfiguration security

[Threat List]
During Configure Verifier, a verifier operator may accidentally or incorrectly configure the system to reference the wrong recognized entity credential as its trust anchor — for example, pointing to a test registry instead of a production registry, or to a registry managed by a different governance authority than intended. As a result, during Validate Credential(s), the verifier will accept credentials from issuers recognized by the wrong registry, potentially granting trust to entities that have not been vetted by the intended governance framework. This is an accidental misconfiguration risk rather than an active attack.
R17. Trust Anchor Verification and Configuration Audits (Reduce)
Verifier implementations display the resolved identity of the configured trust anchor (e.g., the subject DID or name from the recognized entity credential) prominently during setup so that operators can confirm they have the correct registry. Deployment tooling requires explicit operator confirmation when configuring trust anchors in production environments. Governance frameworks provide well-known, stable identifiers for their authoritative registries and distinguish test and production registries clearly. Operators perform periodic audits of verifier system trust anchor configuration as part of routine security reviews.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, E1: List Publisher, E4: Verifier, P3: Configure Verifier, P6: Validate Credential(s), C4: Verifier System
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Elevation of Privilege)

Dependency Threats

3.2.17 T17. Anti-Competitive List Gatekeeping

T17. Anti-Competitive List Gatekeeping market competition

[Threat List]
A first-mover that establishes the initial recognized entity credential list for a market vertical — such as a national business register, an accreditation authority, or an industry consortium — can selectively deny registration to competitors or disfavored entities, creating a de facto monopoly or anti-competitive lock-in. Verifiers that rely exclusively on a single recognized entity credential list become entirely dependent on the list publisher’s goodwill and business interests. Conversely, a list publisher can also be coerced by governments or other powerful actors into revoking the recognition of legitimate entities, effectively censoring them from the ecosystem.
R5. List Pluralism via Multi-Trust-Anchor Configuration (Reduce)
The specification permits and encourages multiple overlapping and competing recognized entity credential registries. Verifiers are configurable with multiple independent trust anchors, so that an entity recognized by any one list from within the configured set is accepted. This prevents any single list publisher from having veto power over ecosystem participation. Ecosystem designers actively encourage multiple independent list publishers to publish recognized entity credentials for the same market vertical, reducing systemic dependency on any single authority.
Affected Components: E1: List Publisher, F2: Issue Recognized Entity Credential, O1: Recognized Entity Credential
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Denial of Service)

3.2.18 T18. Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer Breaks Cryptography

T18. Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer Breaks Cryptography security

[Threat List]
The trust model for recognized entity credentials and dependent verifiable credentials depends on the cryptographic integrity of digital signatures. A sufficiently powerful cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) could break the elliptic-curve and RSA-based signature schemes commonly used today, allowing an attacker to forge signatures on credentials without possessing the private key. If such a CRQC capability becomes available before the ecosystem migrates to post-quantum cryptography, all previously issued credentials and recognition assertions become untrustworthy, and the entire chain of trust underpinning recognized entity infrastructure collapses.
R18. Post-Quantum Cryptography Migration Planning (Reduce)
List publishers and issuers monitor the development of post-quantum cryptographic standards (e.g., NIST PQC finalists) and adopt algorithm agility in credential formats so that signature suites can be swapped without redesigning the entire system. Governance frameworks establish migration timelines for transitioning recognized entity credentials to post-quantum signature schemes. Verifiers are prepared to support hybrid signature verification (classical + post-quantum) during transition periods. All parties are aware that credentials issued today using classical cryptography might be vulnerable to retroactive forgery if harvested and attacked later. For further guidance on defending against credential forgery, including forgery resulting from key compromise enabled by a CRQC, see the Verifiable Credential Forgery Defense v1.0 specification.
Affected Components: O1: Recognized Entity Credential, O2: Dependent Verifiable Credential, E1: List Publisher, E2: Issuer, E4: Verifier, P6: Validate Credential(s)
Threat Taxonomy: STRIDE (Tampering)
